\section{Introduction}

Language models are trained to predict text, even when the task is to choose an action according to what it will cause. Generation handles this problem indirectly. A model assigns probability to candidate words or sentences, and those probabilities become action scores. We ask whether consequence prediction offers a different route: encode the current reasoning state, predict how each candidate phrase changes it, and choose among the predicted outcomes.

Joint-embedding predictive architectures make that decomposition explicit \citep{lecun2022path,assran2023ijepa}. They predict a target representation rather than reconstructing every target token. Action-conditioned variants can serve as latent world models and have supported planning in physical environments \citep{assran2025vjepa2}. Their use for reasoning raises a basic issue. An accurate world model specifies which state follows an action, but a planner also needs to know which successor is preferable.

Transition prediction does not settle that preference. Any invertible change of latent coordinates can preserve the learned dynamics. Unless the change also preserves the planning metric, it can reverse which successor appears closer to a goal. We study the resulting gap between predictive error and planning error.

We introduce Geometric Advantage Ranking (GAR), an objective that compares factual and counterfactual consequences available during training. GAR imposes a local order among actions considered at the same history. Our main architecture scores an action through its predicted successor. A matched direct-GAR control uses the same order supervision while bypassing the successor in the policy path. Their comparison asks whether predictive factorization contributes beyond supervised candidate ranking.

The experiments use stylized arithmetic dependency problems. Every method receives the same shuffled set of currently executable intent phrases. The environment executes the chosen phrase and returns its textual consequence. Fixing the candidate interface removes proposal recall and syntax validity from the comparison. The study therefore concerns conditional planning among feasible language actions, not unrestricted language generation.

Four questions organize the empirical study. RQ1 asks whether an action-conditioned JEPA can plan under the common interface. RQ2 asks whether latent transition prediction is sufficient. RQ3 asks whether GAR produces local order structure that predicts closed-loop success. RQ4 asks what JEPA representations preserve, discard, and expose relative to generative baselines.

The MLP JEPA reaches $43.3\%$ strict success across five seeds. The token LM reaches $42.2\%$, the sentence LM reaches $47.1\%$, and a sentence model with auxiliary latent prediction reaches $52.1\%$. The best strict result therefore belongs to the sentence-plus-latent model. The objective controls address a different question. Factual and counterfactual latent prediction without GAR reaches $18.3\%$, while the complete predictive GAR model reaches $43.3\%$. Counterfactual successor prediction adds a further gain beyond order supervision. Raw successor distance is much weaker than the learned GAR energy in a one-seed local audit.

Controlled-future equivalence defines which language histories may share a state. Exact transition prediction leaves the geometry of inequivalent states ambiguous, whereas correct local action ordering is sufficient for shortest-path planning. GAR targets that order structure. A matched direct scorer, gradient-routing controls, and local geometry audits test whether the benefit enters through predictive geometry, supervised readout, or both.
